The Promise to Ishmael

Muhammad enters the final third of the timeline in a very simple way. To understand his mission, we have to ignore modern politics and look only at his basic life story. He did not come from wealth or education. He was an orphan who grew up in a harsh desert trade environment, and he was almost certainly illiterate. He could not read the Hebrew scriptures or the Christian writings of his time. The fact that an untrained, unlettered man delivered a large and highly structured book—and did so against enormous resistance—is the main sign of his assignment.

And Unlikely Messenger

Because Muhammad had no formal training, the local religious leaders tested him right away. They wanted to prove he was a fraud. The rabbis in the area asked him very specific questions—riddles that only a true prophet could answer. They wanted to know if he really had access to the unseen Source.

The Rabbis’ Test

The first riddle was “Tell us the true story of the People of the Cave.”

The second riddle was ““tell us about the hidden servant of God who traveled with Moses. Who is he, and why did he do the strange things he did?””

The Two Hidden Stories

Muhammad’s answers to the riddles are found in Surah Al‑Kahf, Chapter 18 of the Qur’an. To respond to the rabbis, he described the mysterious servant who traveled with Moses and did strange things that later revealed a hidden purpose. This matches a quiet Jewish story where Elijah guides Rabbi Joshua ben Levi in the same way. The rabbis recognized this as hidden oral lore that an illiterate man in Arabia should not have known. His response showed that he had access to knowledge far beyond his environment.

At the same moment, Muhammad also answered the other riddle by telling the story of the People of the Cave. He described a group of young believers who fled from persecution and fell into a deep sleep inside a cave, waking centuries later without aging. This matches the Christian story of the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus. It was an obscure tradition that an illiterate man in Arabia would not have known on his own.

⭐ The Story from the Midrash Retold by Muhammad

Jewish tradition tells of a journey Rabbi Joshua ben Levi once took with a mysterious traveler. The traveler was Elijah, though Joshua did not know it at first. Elijah agreed to let Joshua walk with him, but only on one condition: Joshua must not question anything he saw along the way. Joshua accepted, and they set out together.

Their first stop was the home of a poor couple who welcomed them with warmth and generosity. They fed the travelers, gave them a place to sleep, and treated them with honor. Yet when Elijah left the next morning, he prayed that the couple’s only cow—their livelihood—should die. Joshua was shaken, but he kept his promise and stayed silent.

Later, they visited a wealthy man who refused them hospitality and treated them with cold indifference. As they departed, Elijah blessed the man’s crumbling wall so it would stand strong. Again, Joshua was confused, but he said nothing.

Finally, unable to contain himself, Joshua begged Elijah to explain why he harmed the kind, helped the cruel, and acted in ways that seemed backwards. Elijah then revealed the hidden logic behind each act. The poor couple’s cow was about to be stolen; losing it early saved them from a greater disaster. The wealthy man’s wall hid a treasure beneath it; strengthening the wall prevented him from finding it.

The moral of the story? Humans don’t know good from evil.

A Limited Mission

Even after hearing both answers, some of the rabbis still held their doubts. But their hesitation did not change Muhammad’s mission or the path he was already on.

His assignment included a period of conflict, and in structure it resembled Joshua’s story: both men were given a specific group to confront, within a specific region, for a specific window of time. But the purpose of their missions was not the same.

  • Joshua’s role was to clear the land of pagan nations so the Israelites could settle there.
  • Muhammad’s role was to call his opponents to accept his message first, and only confront them if they rejected it after repeated offers of peace.

And historically, Joshua’s campaigns resulted in far larger casualties than Muhammad’s far smaller, localized conflict.

Once Muhammad’s mission was completed and the fighting in his region finally ended, he delivered a short and decisive statement that closed the era of religious confrontation. In Surah 109, he declared that faith cannot be forced and that each community is free to follow its own path. This marks the moment when his role shifts from enforcement to peace.

⭐ Muhammad’s Peace Proclamation

In the name of God, the Gracious, the Merciful.

Say, “O disbelievers.

I do not worship what you worship.

Nor do you worship what I worship.

Nor do I serve what you serve.

Nor do you serve what I serve.

You have your way, and I have my way.”

The Larger Pattern

By the time the chapter reaches its end, a pattern has emerged. The rabbis testing Muhammad, the hidden logic in Elijah’s journey, the parallels with Al-Khidr, the limited scope of the conflict, and the final peace proclamation in Surah 109 — each piece points to the same quiet truth. Human beings see only a narrow slice of the timeline. We judge events by their surface, not by their structure.

That is why stories from outside our own tradition can be so unexpectedly clarifying. They don’t replace what we already believe. They simply widen the frame. Elijah’s strange choices make more sense when read beside Khidr’s. Muhammad’s mission becomes easier to understand when placed next to Joshua’s. The echoes across traditions help us see the shape of the story we thought we already knew.

Reading beyond our comfort zone doesn’t pull us away from our faith. It often brings us closer to it. A parallel text can illuminate a familiar one. A symbol from another tradition can sharpen the meaning of our own. And sometimes the clearest way to understand your own scripture is to notice how another tradition preserves the same pattern in a different key.

Nothing in this chapter asks the reader to change their religion. It simply suggests that truth has a way of repeating itself, and that those repetitions become visible only when we allow ourselves to look a little wider.
